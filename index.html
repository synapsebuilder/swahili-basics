    1. Pronunciation is easy

    Read every letter as written. The vowels sound as in German or Italian: a, e, i, o, u. Stress almost always falls on the second to last syllable: a-SA-nte, ta-fa-DHA-li, ki-swa-HI-li.

    dh sounds like "th" in "this". ng' (with the apostrophe) sounds like the end of "sing". r is a light tap.

    2. Giving instructions to the class

    The bare verb is a command to one person. Change the final -a to -eni and you speak to the whole group.

    To oneTo the classMeaning
    SikilizaSikilizenilisten
    AndikaAndikeniwrite
    AngaliaAngalienilook
    KaaKaenisit
    JaribuJaribunitry (verbs ending in -u add -ni)

    The same works for greetings and thanks: Karibu becomes Karibuni, Asante becomes Asanteni, Kwa heri becomes Kwa herini.

    3. "Me", "you" and "them" go inside the verb

    The object slips in right before the verb root.

    Ni-na-ku-penda I like you

    Unaweza ku-ni-saidia? Can you help me?

    Ni-na-wa-fundisha I am teaching them

    Object pieces: -ni- me, -ku- you, -m- him or her, -tu- us, -wa- them.

    4. Nouns come in families

    Each noun belongs to a class, and the class decides its plural and the words that agree with it. Start with the two you will hear most.

    FamilyOneMany"my"
    People (m- / wa-)mwalimu, mwanafunzi, mtotowalimu, wanafunzi, watotomwalimu wangu
    Things (ki- / vi-)kitabu, kiti, kikundivitabu, viti, vikundikitabu changu, vitabu vyangu
    Many others (n-)nyumba, bei, chainyumba, bei, chainyumba yangu
    Some (ji- / ma-)darasa, wazo, swalimadarasa, mawazo, maswalidarasa langu, wazo langu

    If you mix these up, people still understand you. Learn them as fixed pairs ("wazo langu", "kitabu changu") and the pattern settles in by itself.

    5. Asking questions

    Keep normal word order and raise your voice at the end. Question words usually go last.

    Unaenda wapi? Where are you going?

    Hii ni nini? What is this?

    Tunaanza saa ngapi? What time do we start?

    wapi where · nini what · nani who · lini when · ngapi how many · vipi / -je how

    6. Shortcuts you will hear

    Nataka is the everyday form of ninataka (I want). Naomba (I ask for) is the polite way to request anything: "Naomba maji", "Naomba msaada". In the classroom, "Mmeelewa?" (did you understand?) almost always gets a polite "Ndio". Asking students to explain in their own words tells you more.

    Swahili time starts at sunrise

    The Swahili day begins at 7 in the morning, which is saa moja (hour one). Take the clock time and subtract 6 hours. When the students say "saa mbili", they mean 8 o'clock.
